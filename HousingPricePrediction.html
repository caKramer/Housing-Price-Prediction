We can see a right-skew to the data with the higher prices. A skewed distribution will often pull the intercept higher as the model will attempt to find a starting point that balances the high value outliers.

When the dependent variable is skewed, the slopes for variables like area may be exaggerated by the extreme values in the tail, rather than representing the typical home in the dataset.

Linear regression assumes that the residuals are normally distributed. Since the price is heavily skewed the residuals from the model are likely to also be non-normal.

Now let me try a density plot of the area. This is my primary predictor. The model doesn’t require predictors to be normal, but extreme spread can affect teh stability of the model.

I can see a very right-skew in the data here as well so there are few very large properties for sale. This shows the spread in my predictors. Properties in the far-right tail of the area distribution will act as high leverage points. They have a disproportionate influence on the calculation of the \(\beta\) coefficient for area. If these large area homes don’t have a correspondingly large price it will pull the regression line down and weaken the \(\beta\) estimate for the rest of the dataset.

Create a linear regression model where “area” infers price

In R, we use the lm() function to create a linear model. The syntax price ~ area indicates that price is the dependent variable and area is the predictor.

model1 <- lm(price ~ area, data = housing)
summary(model1)

Get a summary of the first model and explain the results

  • Intercept : approximately 2,387,000. Theoretically this represents the baseline price of a property with zero area.

  • Coefficient for Area : This indicates that for every one-unit increase in area the sale price is expected to increase by approximately $462.00, with all other factors being constant.

  • Statistical Significance : Both the intercept and the area variable have p-values of \(<2e-16\) (Indicated by the *** significance code). This means the relationship between area and price is highly statistically significant

  • \(R^2\) Coefficient of Determination : \(\alpha=0.2873\) This means that approximately 28.7% of the variation in house prices can be explained by the size of the area alone

  • Adjusted \(R^2\) : \(\beta = 0.286\) This is similar to \(R^2\), but is adjusted for the number of predictors. This remains close here because we only have one predictor

  • MSE (Mean Square Error) : This is the average of the squared residuals

  • RMSE (Root Mean Squared Error) : This tells me that, on average, the model’s price predictions are off by about 1,581,000

  • F-Statistic : The F-statistic p-value indicates that the model as a whole is a significantly better predictor of price than a model with no predictors (mean only model)

  • Residuals : The residuals range from -4,867,112 to 7,484,838. The fact that the max residual is significantly further from the median than the Min residual indicates a right-skew. This suggests that the model tends to under-predict the price of high-end luxury homes.

Plotting the residuals

res1 <- resid(model1)
plot(fitted(model1), res1, main="Residuals vs Predicted (Model 1)", 
     xlab="Predicted Price", ylab="Residuals")
abline(h = 0, col = "red")

What does the plot tell you about your predictions?

You can see the sort of fanning that is happening as the house prices increase the errors are getting larger. This is a visual of the heteroscendasticity. This suggests that the area alone is not enough to accurately predict high-value homes. This makes sense after seeing the skew and outliers in all of the original plots that I created. The model is trying to balance the high-value outliers and we can see the issues here with the predictions in price.

This fanning indicates that as house prices increase, the model’s error variance increases, which is a common violation of linear regression assumptions in real estate data.

Use a QQ plot to observe your residuals.

qqnorm(res1)
qqline(res1, col = "red")

Do your residuals meet the normality assumption?

The residuals show some deviation from the red line at the tails (ends). This means the residuals are not perfectly normal, most likely due to high-priced outliers in the dataset.
